If you are starting a GLP-1 medication such as semaglutide or tirzepatide, I recommend checking in with your prescribing physician every month while your dose is being adjusted. Each visit is a chance to look at how you are tolerating the medication, how your weight is changing, and whether the dose should go up, stay the same, or come down. Once you reach a maintenance dose, check-ins every three to six months are generally appropriate.

Before any of that, treatment should start with a comprehensive assessment.

## A prescription is not the same as a treatment plan

Simply having a prescription is not enough. These medications work best, and most safely, when someone is actually monitoring how your body responds.

That matters for two reasons. The first is side effects, which can change as your dose changes. The second is the weight loss itself. Losing weight is the goal, but losing it too quickly can cause harm of its own. Monthly check-ins are how those risks are caught early and kept small.

## What the first visit should cover

Treatment should begin with a comprehensive assessment, not a questionnaire alone. At a minimum, that means reviewing:

- **Your weight and BMI**, which serve as the baseline for everything that follows.
- **Your goals**, including what you are hoping to change and why.
- **Your medical history**, including current medications and past surgeries.
- **Conditions that often accompany obesity**, such as high blood pressure, diabetes, and high cholesterol.

These conditions matter because weight loss can change them, sometimes quickly. Medications for blood pressure or blood sugar may need to be reviewed as your weight comes down. The prescribing information for both semaglutide and tirzepatide also advises monitoring blood glucose in people with diabetes, because their diabetes treatment may need to be adjusted.[1][2]

## Why monthly check-ins while the dose is going up

GLP-1 medications are started at a low dose and increased gradually. The FDA-approved schedules for semaglutide and tirzepatide both increase the dose in steps of at least four weeks.[1][2] That timing lines up naturally with a monthly visit.

I adjust the dose month to month until a patient reaches either their maximum tolerated dose or their weight-loss goal. Each step up is a decision, not an automatic refill, and it depends on how the previous dose went.

### Side effects can appear at any dose

This is one of the most important things I've learned from my patients. Many will tell me they tolerated a dose with zero side effects, and then develop them as soon as they move up to the next one. Others have side effects on lower doses and do fine at higher ones. There is no way to predict ahead of time who will react, or at which dose.

The clinical trial data fit that pattern. In the tirzepatide trials, most episodes of nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea occurred during dose escalation and decreased over time.[2] But a trial average can't tell you how *you* will respond. That's why the whole process needs close monitoring by a physician.

### Weight loss itself needs watching

Faster isn't automatically better. When weight comes off quickly, several things can go along with it:

- **Muscle loss.** Some of the weight lost on any weight-loss treatment comes from lean tissue, which includes muscle, not just fat. In a review of 28 clinical trials of GLP-1-based medications, the share of weight lost as fat-free mass ranged from about 20% to 40%, and most trials reported more than 25%.[3]
- **Hair loss.** Hair loss was reported more often with semaglutide and tirzepatide than with placebo in clinical trials, and the prescribing information for both links it to weight reduction.[1][2]
- **Changes in bone density.** Weight loss can be accompanied by a decline in bone density. In one randomized trial, people taking the GLP-1 medication liraglutide alone lost more hip and spine bone density than those who exercised, while people who combined the medication with exercise did not.[4] The evidence for semaglutide and tirzepatide specifically is still limited.
- **Gallbladder problems.** The semaglutide prescribing information notes that substantial or rapid weight loss can increase the risk of gallstones.[1]
- **Cosmetic changes.** Losing weight quickly can also change how your face looks, including a loss of facial fullness that people often call "Ozempic face."

Monthly check-ins help reduce these risks. If weight is coming off faster than is healthy, or a new side effect appears, it can be addressed before it becomes a larger problem.

## A side effect doesn't mean you have to stop

Patients sometimes assume that a side effect means the medication has failed. Usually it doesn't.

Some side effects can be managed with supportive medications. For example, I may prescribe ondansetron for nausea or omeprazole for acid reflux, depending on the situation. In other cases, lowering the dose works better than stopping entirely. A lower dose can still help patients lose weight, and the tirzepatide prescribing information notes that a lower maintenance dose can be considered when a higher one is not tolerated.[2]

The right adjustment depends on the side effect, how severe it is, and your overall health. That decision is one to make with your prescribing clinician, not on your own.

## How I approach this with my patients

Although there have been many studies on the benefits and side effects of medications like tirzepatide and semaglutide, everyone responds to them differently. So I start at the lowest dose and increase it monthly, watching both how well the medication works and how well it is tolerated.

I also tell patients to expect variation in their results. Weight loss differs from person to person, from dose to dose, and over different lengths of treatment. You should not expect to lose all the weight in a short span. Steady, well-tolerated weight loss over a long time is better than short bursts of weight loss.

## What happens once you reach a maintenance dose

Once you reach a maintenance dose, either your maximum tolerated dose or the dose that meets your goal, the pace of change slows. At that point, check-ins every three to six months are generally appropriate.

Those visits still matter. They are a chance to confirm that your weight is stable, review any conditions that have changed with your weight loss, and decide whether your plan still fits your goals.

## When to contact your prescribing clinician

You don't need to wait for your next scheduled visit if something changes. Contact the clinician managing your medication if:

- You develop new side effects after a dose increase.
- Nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea continues rather than easing. The prescribing information advises reporting these promptly, because dehydration from fluid loss can harm the kidneys.[2]
- You are struggling to eat or drink enough.
- You are thinking about stopping the medication, skipping doses, or changing your dose.
- You start or stop another medication, or are planning surgery.

## Symptoms that need prompt medical attention

Some symptoms should not wait for a routine visit. According to the prescribing information, seek prompt medical care for:[1][2]

- Severe abdominal pain that does not go away, with or without vomiting, sometimes spreading to the back. This can be a sign of pancreatitis.
- Symptoms that suggest a gallbladder problem, such as pain in the upper abdomen, fever, yellowing of the skin or eyes, or clay-colored stools.
- Signs of a serious allergic reaction, such as swelling of the face, lips, tongue, or throat, or trouble breathing. Call 911 if breathing becomes difficult.

## Bottom line

A GLP-1 prescription is the start of treatment, not the whole plan. Begin with a comprehensive assessment. Expect to check in every month while your dose is being adjusted, because side effects can appear at any step and weight loss needs to happen at a pace your body tolerates. Once you're on a stable maintenance dose, visits every three to six months are generally appropriate. Throughout treatment, aim for steady, sustainable progress rather than speed.
