When you choose a clinician to prescribe a GLP-1 medication such as semaglutide or tirzepatide, look at two things: their credentials and the breadth of their medical knowledge. Then ask whether they will actually monitor you, not just write the prescription.

The right background depends on your health. If you have several chronic conditions, such as high blood pressure, diabetes, high cholesterol, or liver disease, and you take many medications, a physician with an internal medicine background may suit you better. If fertility or pregnancy is part of the picture, a clinician with an OB/GYN background may be a better match. A physician certified by the American Board of Obesity Medicine (ABOM) has completed obesity-specific training and passed an examination in comprehensive weight-management care, whatever their original specialty.

## Prescribing is not the same as monitoring

This is the distinction I see patients miss most often.

Signing off on a prescription is easy. The hard part comes after it. Someone has to watch for side effects, judge how well you are tolerating the medication, and adjust the dose of semaglutide or tirzepatide over time so you can actually reach your weight-loss goals.

That work gets more involved when your medical history is complex. If you have other conditions and take other medications, your clinician has to work out what is causing a new symptom. It could be the GLP-1 medication, the treatment for another condition, or the condition itself. Getting that wrong can mean stopping a medication that was working, or missing a problem that needed attention.

## What credentials to look for

**Board certification in obesity medicine.** ABOM certification is open to physicians who already hold board certification in another specialty. To qualify, a physician must also complete either an obesity medicine fellowship or at least 60 credits of obesity-focused continuing medical education, and then pass the certification exam. ABOM describes certification as signifying "specialized knowledge in the practice of obesity medicine." You can check whether a physician is certified using ABOM's public diplomate search.

In my view, this is the most useful single credential to look for. It shows that a physician has deliberately trained in treating obesity rather than prescribing these medications as a side task.

**A background that fits your health history.** Obesity is a complex metabolic disease, and different organ systems interact in ways that affect treatment. A clinician whose training matches your other conditions will be better prepared to handle that interplay.

## Why the care model matters as much as the credential

Many telehealth companies write a prescription and then leave patients on their own. Patients deal with side effects, figure out dose increases, and turn to apps or online communities to piece the process together. That often leads to confusion and frustration.

Some platforms assign you to a physician or another licensed prescriber, such as a nurse practitioner or physician assistant, who approves medications based on a questionnaire. A video visit may or may not be offered. In some cases these clinicians are third-party contractors rather than part of the company itself. When that happens, the company and the clinician have different priorities. The company's business depends on medication sales. The contracted clinician has their own license to protect and may be cautious about approving prescriptions based on limited information. This is one reason some people sign up and pay, only to have the medication denied.

Rotating clinicians cause a related problem. If you see whoever is available rather than someone dedicated to your case, the advice you get is likely to be generic, because that clinician doesn't know your history.

## Don't choose on price alone

Price matters, and I take cost seriously when I build a treatment plan. But choosing a medication deal over a clinician can cost more in the end.

Some companies offer bulk pricing, such as a discounted three-month supply of tirzepatide at the starting dose. That can look like savings. But many patients need a higher dose before those three months are up, either because they plateau or because the starting dose was never meant to be the long-term dose. Then you are paying for new medication on top of what you already bought.

More medication is not the same as more cost-effective treatment. In my experience, patients who choose a medication offer over a physician often spend more and still don't reach their goal, because they are working in the dark.

Many people stop GLP-1 treatment early. In one large study of U.S. adults with overweight or obesity, about 65% of those without type 2 diabetes stopped their GLP-1 medication within a year. Those who had moderate or severe gastrointestinal side effects were more likely to stop, and those who lost more weight were less likely to stop. That study didn't look at what kind of clinician patients had. Still, I believe a lack of ongoing, informed follow-up is part of why so many people stop.

## How I approach this with my patients

I tell patients that weight loss is a marathon, not a sprint.

Continuity is central to how I practice. Knowing a patient's health history and medications is what lets me build a plan that is both safe and effective. It's also what lets me tell whether a new symptom is a medication side effect, something related to another condition, or something else entirely.

With appropriate monitoring and a personalized plan from an experienced physician, weight-loss goals are achievable. Results vary from person to person, and the plan should adapt as you go.

## When to contact your prescribing clinician

If you have side effects, tell your prescribing clinician promptly. With my own patients, I want to hear about it right away, by portal message or phone call, if they have:

- Nausea
- Vomiting
- Diarrhea
- Constipation
- Acid reflux

Many of these side effects are temporary and manageable. Your clinician's job is to triage them: decide whether a symptom is likely to pass on its own, whether it needs treatment, or whether it needs urgent attention.

If your current clinician isn't reachable when you have questions like these, consider that a sign to look at your options.

## Bottom line

A good GLP-1 prescriber does more than approve a medication. When you compare options, ask:

- Is the clinician board certified, ideally in obesity medicine?
- Does their background fit your health history and other medications?
- Will you see the same clinician over time, or whoever is available?
- Who adjusts your dose, and how?
- How do you reach them when you have a side effect?
- Is the price a short-term medication deal, or the cost of actual care?
