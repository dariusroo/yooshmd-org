If you have started a GLP-1 medication such as semaglutide or tirzepatide and haven't lost much weight, it doesn't necessarily mean the medication has failed. Response to these medications is highly variable. Age, sex, diabetes, and other medical conditions all influence how your body responds. Side effects can also slow the move to higher doses, and that can limit weight loss along the way. The honest answer is that no one can know your outcome in advance. It becomes clear only after you start the medication and go through appropriate clinical monitoring.

## Trial results are averages, not predictions

The large clinical trials of these medications showed substantial weight loss. In adults without diabetes, people taking semaglutide 2.4 mg lost an average of 14.9% of their body weight over 68 weeks, compared with 2.4% on placebo.[1] In a similar trial of tirzepatide, average weight loss over 72 weeks was 15.0%, 19.5%, and 20.9% at the 5 mg, 10 mg, and 15 mg doses, compared with 3.1% on placebo.[2]

Those numbers are averages. Within every trial, some people lost much more and some lost much less. A trial result tells you what is possible across a large group. It can't tell you how *you* will respond, or how quickly.

## Weight loss doesn't come from the medication alone

A common misunderstanding is that weight loss is tied only to the medication. It isn't. Diet and exercise are also important, and they shape how well the medication works. Both semaglutide and tirzepatide are FDA-approved for weight management in combination with a reduced-calorie diet and increased physical activity,[3][4] and participants in the major trials received lifestyle counseling alongside the medication.[1][2]

The other factors I mentioned, such as age, sex, and coexisting medical conditions, also influence response. Put together, this is why response is so variable from one person to the next.

## Why some people respond more slowly

### Diabetes

From my own experience, patients with diabetes tend to lose less weight and need higher doses to reach their goals. The clinical trials point the same way. In adults with type 2 diabetes, semaglutide 2.4 mg produced an average weight loss of 9.6% over 68 weeks,[5] lower than the 14.9% seen in people without diabetes.[1] Tirzepatide produced average losses of 12.8% and 14.7% at the 10 mg and 15 mg doses in people with type 2 diabetes,[6] again lower than in the trial without diabetes.[2]

### Prior gastric bypass surgery

In my practice, patients who have had gastric bypass surgery also tend to lose less weight and need higher doses. This is my clinical observation. Research on GLP-1 medications after bariatric surgery is more limited.

### Side effects that slow the dose increases

These medications are started at a low dose and increased in steps. For both semaglutide and tirzepatide, the FDA-approved schedule increases the dose no faster than every four weeks.[3][4] If side effects make a dose hard to tolerate, the semaglutide prescribing information suggests considering a delay in the next dose increase,[3] and the tirzepatide prescribing information suggests considering a lower maintenance dose.[4] Either can be the right call for safety, but it can also mean it takes longer to reach, or never reach, the higher doses.

## A slow start or a plateau isn't the end of the story

Some of my patients are initially frustrated when they don't lose weight at the lower doses, or when they hit a plateau. But as they approach higher doses and stay on them over the long term, they eventually see improvements. A slow first few months is not, by itself, a reason to conclude the medication isn't working.

## Don't rush the dose

When weight loss is slow, it's tempting to jump to a higher dose. That is risky. Skipping steps in the titration schedule can bring on dangerous side effects. Titration should follow the FDA-recommended schedule, and if you aren't responding at the lower doses, that is not a reason to rush to the highest one.

Any change to your dose should be a decision you make with your prescribing clinician.

## Not responding to one GLP-1 doesn't mean nothing will work

If a GLP-1 truly isn't working for you, that doesn't mean you won't respond to any weight-loss medication. There are several other paths:

- **Switching to a different GLP-1.** Some people switch GLP-1 medications and end up seeing good results.
- **Trying a non-GLP-1 medication.** Some patients respond better to medications such as phentermine, topiramate, or bupropion. Phentermine-topiramate and naltrexone-bupropion are combination medications that are FDA-approved for chronic weight management.
- **Combining medications.** A GLP-1 can also be combined with a non-GLP-1 medication to boost its effect. The evidence for specific combinations is still limited, and this is a decision that needs careful physician oversight.

Which option makes sense depends on your health history, your other medications, and how you responded to what you have already tried.

## How I approach this with my patients

I rely on rigorous clinical monitoring. Regular check-ins help optimize how long a patient stays on the medication, when doses change, and what treatment costs. I adjust doses based on each patient's goal weight and their side effects. That approach helps improve outcomes while limiting adverse effects.

In practice, that means I don't judge response on the first dose or the first few weeks. I look at how weight is changing as the dose increases, how well each dose is tolerated, and the patient's own health factors, such as diabetes or prior surgery, that may call for a higher dose or more time.

## When to contact your prescribing clinician

Contact the clinician managing your medication if:

- You've been on treatment for a while and are concerned your weight loss has stalled or isn't happening.
- Side effects are making it hard to stay on your current dose or move to the next one.
- You're thinking about changing your dose, skipping doses, switching medications, or stopping.
- You start or stop another medication, including other weight-loss medications.

## Bottom line

Weight loss on a GLP-1 medication varies widely. Diabetes, prior gastric bypass, age, sex, and other conditions can all slow your response, and side effects can delay the dose increases where much of the weight loss happens. Slow early progress or a plateau doesn't automatically make you a non-responder. Many people improve as they reach higher doses and stay on them. If a medication truly isn't working, switching to another GLP-1, trying a non-GLP-1 medication, or combining medications are all options. The way to find out is steady titration and careful monitoring with your physician, not rushing the dose.
